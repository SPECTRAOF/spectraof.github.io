Trading Fundamentals

What is a market?

A market is an auction where participants continuously submit orders to buy and sell. Price changes when available liquidity is consumed or when participants reprice their orders.

Key idea: price is the result of transactions and order placement, not an isolated line moving by itself.

Bid, ask and spread

The bid is the highest displayed price someone is willing to buy at. The ask is the lowest displayed price someone is willing to sell at. The difference is the spread.

Market orders generally execute against available liquidity. Limit orders add or wait for liquidity at a chosen price.

Market orders vs limit orders

A market order prioritizes execution. A limit order prioritizes price. Neither is automatically better: the correct choice depends on the execution problem you are solving.

ExampleYou want to buy immediately → execution certainty matters more.You only want to buy at 100.00 or lower → price control matters more.

Reading Price

What a candle actually tells you

Open, high, low and close describe the range and final position of price during a period. A candle is a compressed record of the auction, not a complete explanation of why price moved.

Swings and market structure

Instead of treating every candle as equally important, identify meaningful swing highs and lows. Structure gives context to individual movements and helps distinguish continuation from a change in behavior.

Trend, range and transition

A market can broadly be expanding in one direction, rotating inside a range, or transitioning between the two. The important question is not simply “up or down?” but what is price doing relative to the structure that came before it?

Risk & Psychology

Risk comes before the setup

A trading idea can be correct and still produce a losing trade. Risk management separates the quality of an idea from the size of its consequence.

Define risk per trade, maximum daily loss and conditions under which you stop trading before entering.

Expectancy

Expectancy describes the average outcome of a strategy over many trades. A simple model is:

Expectancy = (Win rate × average win) − (Loss rate × average loss)

A strategy does not need to win most trades to have positive expectancy if its winners are sufficiently larger than its losers.

Drawdown and the losing streak

Short sequences of losses are normal in probabilistic systems. The goal is to size risk so that an ordinary losing sequence does not force you to abandon the process.

Building a Process

Turn an idea into a rule

“I buy when the market looks strong” is difficult to test. A process becomes testable when context, setup, trigger, invalidation and exit are defined clearly enough that another person could identify the same trade.

Backtesting

Backtesting asks whether a defined rule set produced a repeatable distribution of outcomes on historical data. Record every qualifying trade, not only the attractive ones.

Journal what matters

Track setup, context, entry, stop, target, result, execution quality and deviations from the plan. The journal should help answer what is actually happening?, not simply how you felt about the last trade.

Market Structure

Structure is context

Market structure organizes price into meaningful swings and areas of acceptance or rejection. A footprint signal without context can mean something very different inside a range than at a structural extreme.

Balance and imbalance

Balance describes two-sided trade and repeated acceptance around an area. Imbalance describes a stronger directional auction where one side controls the progression of price.

Acceptance vs rejection

A quick move through a level is not the same as sustained trade there. Ask whether the market is actually accepting prices beyond a level or rejecting them and returning to the prior area.

Volume

Volume: activity, not direction

Volume tells you how much activity occurred, but volume alone does not tell you who will win next. Its meaning depends on price response, location and context.

Volume Profile

Volume Profile distributes activity across price levels rather than time. Common references include POC, the price with the greatest volume in the profile, and VAH / VAL, the upper and lower boundaries of the selected value area.

ExampleA large volume node can show an area where the market previously spent significant activity.A low-volume area can represent a thinner part of the distribution where price previously moved through with less activity.

The exact calculation depends on the data and implementation. TradingView notes that its Volume Profile calculations use lower-timeframe data and that volume type differs across instruments. Source: TradingView

POC, VAH, VAL, HVN and LVN

These are references, not automatic trade signals. Their usefulness comes from observing how current price behaves when it interacts with previously active or thin areas.

Order Flow

What order flow adds

Traditional candles summarize price over time. Order flow can expose the transactions and aggressor-side activity occurring inside that summary, depending on the quality and granularity of the feed.

Footprint charts

A footprint can display traded volume at individual price levels, often separated into bid and ask activity. This makes it possible to study where aggressive buying or selling occurred inside a candle.

Delta

A common order-flow delta is aggressive buy volume minus aggressive sell volume. Positive delta means more volume was classified on the buy side; negative delta means more was classified on the sell side.

Read the combinationPositive delta + strong upward price response → aggression is producing movement.Positive delta + little upward progress → investigate whether that effort is being absorbed.

Liquidity

What liquidity means

Liquidity is the availability of orders that can be matched with incoming demand or supply. A visible order book shows displayed liquidity, while executed trades show what actually transacted.

DOM and market depth

The DOM can show resting bids and asks around the current market. Depth is useful for understanding the current order-book state, but displayed orders can change or disappear and should not automatically be treated as committed intent.

Liquidity interaction

When aggressive orders consume resting liquidity, price may move to the next available level. Watch how price responds after liquidity is consumed: continuation, rejection and replenishment tell different stories.

Market Microstructure

The matching process

Electronic markets match compatible orders according to the rules of the venue. Price and time priority are common principles, but the exact mechanism depends on the exchange and product.

Displayed vs executed liquidity

Displayed depth is a snapshot. Executed volume is evidence of a completed transaction. Keeping these concepts separate prevents you from treating every visible order as equally meaningful.

Queue, spread and execution cost

Execution quality depends on spread, available depth, queue position, volatility and the behavior of the book while your order is waiting. The same entry price can have very different execution quality under different conditions.

Advanced Order Flow

Absorption

Absorption is a framework for describing aggressive buying or selling that meets sufficient opposing liquidity so price makes limited progress. It is not simply “a big number on the footprint”. Location and subsequent price response matter.

Exhaustion

Exhaustion describes a reduction in aggressive participation near a move's extreme. It can be relevant when aggressive activity stops producing continuation, but it should be interpreted with structure and context.

Effort vs Result

This is one of the most useful ways to connect volume and price. Effort is the amount of activity or aggression. Result is what price actually accomplishes.

100buyers pushing
VS
300sellers pushing

If a very large amount of selling effort produces only a small downward result, something is limiting that pressure. Conversely, strong effort followed by strong displacement suggests that the effort is actually producing movement.

Question to ask: “How much effort was required, and what did price achieve with it?”

CVD & Divergence

What is CVD?

Cumulative Volume Delta accumulates delta through time. In a common implementation, it represents cumulative aggressive buy volume minus aggressive sell volume. Bookmap describes CVD as a way to track the balance between aggressive buyers and sellers over time. Source: Bookmap

Price vs CVD divergence

A divergence occurs when price and CVD move differently. For example, price can make a new high while CVD fails to make a corresponding high. This can reveal a change in aggressive participation, but it is not a standalone reversal signal.

Use CVD as confirmation

CVD becomes more useful when combined with structure, liquidity and price response. Bookmap explicitly presents CVD as a confirmation tool rather than something to use alone for entries. Source: Bookmap

Execution & Process

Analysis is not execution

A good read of the market does not guarantee a good fill. Separate the analytical thesis from the mechanics of entering, managing and exiting the position.

Slippage and market conditions

Slippage is the difference between an expected execution price and the actual fill. It can increase when volatility rises, liquidity thins or the market moves quickly.

Build evidence, not stories

After a trade, compare what you expected with what actually happened. Use a large enough sample to distinguish a repeatable characteristic from a memorable single event.
